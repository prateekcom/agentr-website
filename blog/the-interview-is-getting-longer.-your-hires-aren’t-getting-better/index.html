The Interview Is Getting Longer. Your Hires Aren't Getting Better.

Offer acceptance has fallen to 51%, and technical roles now average 17.6 rounds. The best candidates walk out before the process ends.

Prateek Porwal · 11 May 2026 · 7 min read

on this page

There is a belief, largely unexamined, sitting at the centre of how most companies hire.

The belief is this: more interviews mean better decisions. If one conversation isn't enough to assess someone, add another. If four rounds aren't conclusive, schedule a fifth. If the team is still uncertain, bring in a panel. The instinct to add evaluation is so deeply embedded in corporate hiring culture that questioning it feels almost reckless — like suggesting you should make a major financial decision with less due diligence, not more.

But the data says something different. And the gap between what companies believe about interview volume and what the research actually shows is one of the most expensive misalignments in modern hiring.

The numbers don't lie

Start with the macro trend.

The average hiring process now spans 68.5 days, according to 2025 research. For senior and specialised positions, it regularly exceeds 90. That's not a temporary anomaly — it represents a structural shift in how long companies have decided it should take to make a hiring decision.

The interview load has followed a parallel trajectory. Technical roles now average 17.6 interviews per hire — a 52% increase from 2021, when the same roles averaged around 11. Data roles have climbed to 19.5 interviews per hire. Product management sits at 18. Engineering at 17.9.

These numbers aren't the result of any single decision. They are the accumulated product of thousands of small additions — a VP who asked to be included, a peer panel that became standard practice, a "culture fit" screen layered on top of a technical assessment that was layered on top of a phone screen that was layered on top of an initial recruiter call. Each addition felt justified in isolation. The cumulative effect is a process that takes the better part of three months to complete.

And candidates are leaving before it does.

The attrition the interview generates

Here is the finding that should stop any talent leader cold.

Offer acceptance fell to 51% in Q2 2025, down from 74% just two years prior. That means roughly one in every two candidates who received a job offer said no. Not because the compensation was wrong. Not because they found something better. In 36% of cases, the reason was a negative experience during the interview process itself.

The interview — the process designed to find the right candidate — is causing the right candidate to decline.

The attrition starts earlier than the offer stage. Forty-two percent of candidates withdrew from hiring processes specifically because scheduling took too long. Another 47% cited poor communication as their reason for disengaging. The interview stage now accounts for 32% of all candidate drop-off — more than application abandonment, scheduling delays, and onboarding friction combined.

A quarter of candidates disengage between the interview and the offer. They made it all the way through the process and still left.

There is no other function in a well-run organisation that would accept a 49% conversion rate on its most important output and respond by adding complexity to the workflow. Hiring does it routinely — and calls the added complexity "rigour."

The Google finding that changed nothing

In 2016, Google's internal people analytics team published a finding that should have restructured how every organisation thinks about interview volume.

After analysing years of hiring data, the research concluded that four interviews are sufficient to predict with 86% confidence whether a candidate is a qualified hire. More than four interviews generated no statistically meaningful additional information about the candidate. The marginal value of the fifth, sixth, or seventh interview was, in the data, essentially zero.

Google capped its standard process at four rounds. It was a research-based decision made by a company with more hiring data than almost any organisation on earth.

And then every other company in the industry kept adding rounds anyway.

The research didn't travel, because the belief it challenged was too fundamental. "More evaluation is better" isn't a policy position that most companies have consciously adopted. It's an ambient assumption so deeply embedded in hiring culture that a single study — even a Google study — couldn't dislodge it.

The result is an industry that knows diminishing returns exist, has evidence of where they begin, and continues to schedule around them.

What the interviews are actually measuring

The harder question behind interview volume is what the interviews themselves are measuring.

The meta-analytic record on this is not new, but it's also not widely internalised. Unstructured interviews — the conversational, "tell me about yourself," intuition-based formats that still dominate most hiring processes — carry a predictive validity of approximately 0.38 for job performance. Structured interviews, which use consistent questions evaluated against a predetermined framework, reach approximately 0.51.

Both of these are better than chance. Neither of them is high. And the difference — the gap between the unstructured interview most companies are running and the structured interview the research recommends — is not compensated for by adding more rounds of the same format.

Running six unstructured interviews doesn't produce the validity of six structured interviews. It produces the variance of six different conversations by six different people forming six different impressions, none of which is systematically comparable to the others.

The interrater reliability of an unstructured panel is low. Two people interviewing the same candidate in the same day can reach opposite conclusions and both feel confident in their judgment. Adding more interviewers doesn't resolve this problem. It multiplies it.

This is why 52% of candidates say that four to five rounds is too many, and why roughly a third say that even two to three rounds crosses a line. Candidates have an intuitive sense that something isn't working — that the additional scrutiny isn't producing better decisions, just more friction. The data supports their intuition.

The candidate the long process loses

Every week of process length creates selection pressure. Not for the most qualified candidates — for the most available ones.

The candidate who can wait 68.5 days for a hiring decision is, by definition, not the candidate currently receiving other offers. The passive candidate who agreed to explore a conversation, then attended three rounds, then found their enthusiasm for the role had cooled by the time scheduling for round four began — that candidate is gone. They didn't reject the company formally. They just stopped responding.

The research is fairly direct about who walks away from long processes. Top performers — candidates with strong track records who are being actively recruited, who have multiple processes running in parallel — disengage earlier and at higher rates than average candidates. They have less tolerance for disorganised scheduling, less patience for redundant rounds, and more options to exercise when a process signals that their time isn't valued.

The long process doesn't filter for quality. It filters for availability. And availability is not a proxy for capability.

The accountability gap behind the escalation

If the research is clear and the candidate behaviour is documented, why does interview volume keep growing?

The answer is incentive design.

Adding an interview round is low-risk for everyone who participates in making that decision. The hiring manager who requests another panel has protected themselves — if the hire goes wrong, they can say they were thorough. The recruiter who schedules an additional loop has reduced their exposure. The HR team that adds a cultural assessment to an already-extended process is demonstrating diligence.

Nobody owns the cost of the candidate who dropped out in round five. Nobody is accountable for the offer acceptance rate from a process they extended by three weeks. The harm from a long, friction-heavy interview falls on the candidate — and, eventually, on the hire quality data — but it doesn't surface in a way that any individual decision-maker has to answer for.

This is how a problem that everyone can see at an aggregate level persists at an individual level. The incentives aren't aligned with the outcome.

What the exit looks like

The solution is not a shorter bad process. Adding speed to an unstructured, inconsistently evaluated, poorly designed interview loop just loses candidates faster without improving quality.

The exit is the combination that the research has consistently pointed toward: fewer, structured interviews designed to gather specific evidence of capability, with a clear decision framework that means each round adds genuinely new information rather than repeating the previous one.

Four rounds maximum, with a predetermined question structure and a calibrated scoring approach, will outperform eight intuition-led conversations both in predictive validity and in the candidates it retains to the offer stage.

But interview structure alone doesn't solve the underlying measurement problem. The interview — even a well-constructed one — is still asking candidates to describe what they've done and could do in a conversation setting. It doesn't assess what their career actually looks like: the trajectory of decisions they made, the contexts they operated in, the progression that either shows sustained growth or reveals a pattern the document has been written to obscure.

Career-pattern evidence is what structured interviews were built to approximate. Evaluating it directly — looking at the sequence of roles, the decisions at each transition, the coherence of what someone built over time — adds a layer of signal that no number of conversation rounds can generate.

The companies that are winning the talent competition in 2026 are not the ones running the most thorough processes. They are the ones running the fastest accurate ones — where every step gathers new evidence, where the decision framework is clear before the process starts, and where strong candidates are moved to an offer before a competitor finishes scheduling their third round.

Rigour is not volume. That's the distinction the hiring industry has been slow to internalise — and the one that the data, and the candidates leaving the process early, are making unavoidable.
