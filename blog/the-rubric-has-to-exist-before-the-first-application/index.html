The rubric has to exist before the first application

Most hiring arguments are about who to hire. The more useful question is when you decided what you were looking for — and almost everyone decides far too late.

Prateek · 4 September 2026 · 3 min read

on this page
  1. A criterion invented on application forty-one
  2. Where "culture fit" hides

Ask a hiring manager what they want and they will tell you, fluently, for ten minutes. Ask them to write it down before they have seen a single application and something changes. The list gets shorter. The adjectives fall away. What survives is usually four or five things that can actually be evidenced.

That gap — between what we can describe and what we are willing to commit to in advance — is where most bad hires are made.

A criterion invented on application forty-one

Here is the failure, stated plainly. A requirement that appears while you are reading the forty-first application is not a requirement. It is a description of applicant forty-one.

This is not dishonesty, and it is not a character flaw. It is how judgment works when it has nothing to push against. Faced with a strong candidate, we reverse-engineer the reason they are strong and promote it to a standard. Faced with a weak one, we find the missing thing and call it essential. Both feel like discernment. Neither is.

Every hiring process has a rubric. The only question is whether it was written before the applications or assembled from them afterwards.

Where "culture fit" hides

The clearest symptom is the criterion nobody can define until it is needed. Culture fit is the usual one. It is rarely a lie — teams do differ, and fit is real — but as an unwritten standard it does no work except to license a decision already made. Written down in advance, it either becomes something specific and testable, or it quietly disappears. Both outcomes are an improvement.

[Bulleted list]

  • Criteria that can be evidenced from something a candidate produces or says, not inferred from where they have worked
  • A stated weight for each, decided before you know who is strong on what
  • A defined range — what a weak, adequate and exceptional answer looks like, written out
  • An explicit list of what you are not assessing, which is the part everyone skips

[Sub-heading]
The uncomfortable part

[Paragraph]
Done properly, a rubric will occasionally rank someone above the candidate you liked. This is the entire point, and it is the reason most teams abandon the practice within two hires. The correct response is not to override the ranking silently. It is to either accept it, or amend the rubric and say what changed — in that order:

[Numbered list]

  1. Write the criteria and weights before the role opens
  2. Score every applicant against them, including the ones you already like
  3. When the result surprises you, decide whether the rubric was wrong or you were
  4. If the rubric was wrong, change it in writing and rescore — do not make an exception

[Callout]
Step four is the whole discipline. An exception made once is a precedent; a rubric amended once is still a rubric.

[Paragraph]
None of this requires software. A shared document and the discipline to fill it in first will do. What software changes is scale: the difficulty is not writing one rubric, it is applying the same one to the thousandth application as carefully as to the first, and being able to show, afterwards, why each person was ranked where they were. [Paragraph]
Write it down first. Everything else in hiring gets easier, and the arguments you do have become arguments about criteria — which are worth having — instead of arguments about people, which usually are not.
